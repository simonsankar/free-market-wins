# Reasoning patterns

The recurring shapes that bad arguments take. Each brief names the patterns its
claims run on, so this file doubles as a fallacy textbook built from real
cases. Add to it when a new shape recurs; give every entry a real example.

Format: **shape** (the structure), **tell** (how to spot it), **answer**,
**rung** (from `descent.md`).

---

## Freeze-and-extrapolate
- **Shape:** one input gets cheap or powerful → assume it was the thing holding
  everything up → assume nobody responds → conclude collapse → hand the fix to
  government. Includes the lump-of-labour fallacy (a fixed amount of work to
  go around).
- **Tell:** a trend line extended to its end with every other variable held
  still. "In ten years there won't be any…"
- **Answer:** name what else moves. Wants are unlimited, so a cheaper input
  shifts scarcity to the next bottleneck rather than ending it; people respond
  to changed conditions, as every previous automated layer shows.
- **Rung:** Economics: Scarcity → Economics: Action. Vault: [[Lump of Labour]].
- **Example:** "AI will make engineers obsolete"; "AI reverse engineering
  means security is dead, government must act."
- **Strong form to expect:** "this time the machine substitutes for cognition
  itself, so there's no higher rung." History of automated layers doesn't
  answer that; the horse argument (horses had comparative advantage too) is the
  real test. Answer: humans are the ends production serves and can own the
  machines; horses were neither. "Only acting beings value" is a priori, not a
  forecast. If they raise concentrated ownership or housing costs, that's
  fiat money and the Cantillon effect, not AI.
- **Defence variant:** the attacker gets the new capability and the defenders
  are frozen. "AI will kill security," "AI makes bioweapons easy." The same
  tool arms the defenders, who vastly outnumber attackers. The proven mass
  killers are states, so a licensing regime hands the gate to them.

## Seen and unseen
- **Shape:** count the visible benefit of an intervention, ignore what the
  resources would otherwise have done (Bastiat's broken window).
- **Tell:** jobs "created" or "saved," with no line for what they cost.
- **Answer:** ask what was given up, and who gave it up.
- **Rung:** Economics: Scarcity. Vault: [[Seen and Unseen]].
- **Example:** tariffs "protecting" factory jobs.

## Nirvana comparison
- **Shape:** the market's real record against the state's ideal.
- **Tell:** "under a proper system…" for one side, actual outcomes for the
  other. Moe Boy's asymmetric failure attribution.
- **Answer:** compare real to real, or ideal to ideal.
- **Rung:** Economics: Incentives.
- **Example:** "private healthcare leaves people out" vs. a public system
  described by its intentions, not its waiting lists.

## Definitional smuggling (motte-and-bailey)
- **Shape:** stretch a word to cover its opposite, then defend the stretched
  version with the original's reputation. The easy-to-defend motte gets swapped
  for the bailey when nobody's checking.
- **Tell:** "capitalism, but with…"; "free" healthcare.
- **Answer:** pin the definition first, in terms the other side accepts, and
  hold it.
- **Rung:** Policy → Rights.
- **Example:** "Nordic countries prove capitalism and a welfare state go
  together." A tax-funded welfare state is the coercive part, not capitalism.

## Golden-age nostalgia
- **Shape:** a past arrangement looked good, so recreate its surface features
  by force.
- **Tell:** "back when we used to make things here."
- **Answer:** what changed, and why? Restoring the surface without the
  conditions means paying more for less, and shrinking the set of trading
  partners worsens scarcity rather than easing it.
- **Rung:** Economics: Scarcity.
- **Example:** protectionism to "bring manufacturing home".

## Zero-sum framing
- **Shape:** one side's gain is the other's loss.
- **Tell:** profit as taking; trade deficits as "losing."
- **Answer:** voluntary exchange happens only when both sides expect to gain.
- **Rung:** Economics: Action.
- **Example:** "the tech oligarchs get rich off us."

## Arbitrary line
- **Shape:** aggression is wrong, except against the people or in the amount
  the speaker doesn't mind.
- **Tell:** "I'm for X, but…"; a threshold with nothing underneath it ("earn
  over $500k, owe 35%").
- **Answer:** ask what the line is derived from. It's [[Mixed Law]].
- **Rung:** Equality.
- **Example:** "Tax the billionaires," from someone who'd call the same taking
  theft if done to them.

## Status quo as default
- **Shape:** whatever currently holds power is presumed right; the challenger
  carries the whole burden of proof.
- **Tell:** government failure gets context, market failure gets a verdict.
  Moe Boy's tactic #1.
- **Answer:** existence isn't justification. Ask for the argument, not the
  precedent.
- **Rung:** Authority → Equality.

## Collective actor
- **Shape:** "we," "America," or "society" chooses, builds, or needs.
- **Tell:** a nation described as if it had one will.
- **Answer:** only individuals act. Ask who chooses and who pays.
- **Rung:** Economics: Action.
- **Example:** "America should build everything in America."

## Benefit without cost
- **Shape:** the good is listed, the bill isn't.
- **Tell:** "free," "guaranteed," "the government provides."
- **Answer:** [[No such thing as a free lunch]]: someone produced it, so who,
  and did they agree?
- **Rung:** Policy → Economics: Scarcity.

## Majority sanction
- **Shape:** it's legitimate because it was voted for.
- **Answer:** a majority can't hand over a right none of its voters have.
- **Rung:** Authority.

## Lateral pivot
- **Shape:** when one claim is answered, move to an adjacent one rather than
  conceding. Moe Boy's tactic #5.
- **Tell:** a new topic arrives as soon as the previous one closes, with the
  same conclusion each time.
- **Answer:** don't chase the leaves. Name the root premise the pivots share
  and answer that once.
- **Rung:** same rung as the root premise.
- **Example:** AI jobs → AI security → data-centre land use, all ending in
  "government should step in".

## Arsonist as fire brigade
- **Shape:** the grievance is caused by a state privilege; the remedy offered
  is more state power.
- **Tell:** a real complaint about a rigged market, with "regulate it" as the
  fix.
- **Answer:** concede the grievance, name the privilege behind it (franchise,
  exemption, permit, subsidy), and remove that instead.
- **Rung:** Economics: Incentives → Equality. Vault: [[Regulatory Capture]].
- **Example:** data centres pushing grid costs onto households through
  commission-set rates, answered with a moratorium.

## Permission before action
- **Shape:** prior licensing proposed where liability after the fact already
  exists.
- **Tell:** "guardrails," "pre-approval," "age verification for everyone."
- **Answer:** liability puts skin in the game on whoever caused the harm;
  licensing restrains everyone and exposes the regulator to nothing.
- **Rung:** Economics: Incentives → Rights. Vault: [[Liability and Restitution]].
- **Example:** the GUARD Act's blanket chatbot ban for minors.

## Tool blame
- **Shape:** restrict everyone who holds a means instead of the person who
  aggresses with it.
- **Tell:** the remedy binds the compliant majority and misses the aggressor.
- **Answer:** prosecute the aggressor; licensing binds the law-abiding, not
  states or black markets. Someone already willing to commit murder isn't
  deterred by a possession charge. Information doesn't aggress, people do, and
  more of it in more hands favours the side with more people on it: the
  defenders.
- **Rung:** Policy → Rights.
- **Example:** banning guns to stop school shootings; licensing AI models to
  stop bioterror; export controls as the answer to deepfake fraud.

## Unfalsifiable gate
- **Shape:** a ban lifted on a condition with no defined test and nobody
  empowered to declare it met.
- **Tell:** "until there's consensus it's safe," "until the public agrees."
- **Answer:** ask what evidence would lift it, and who decides.
- **Rung:** Authority.
- **Example:** the FLI superintelligence prohibition.

## Special case
- **Shape:** "principles are fine normally, but this situation is too
  dangerous / unique / catastrophic, so intervention is necessary."
- **Tell:** extinction, pandemics, "irreversible," "we can't wait for
  lawsuits," "you accept self-defence, and this is a threat."
- **Answer:** no situation is unique enough. The proposal is always a group
  introducing conflict because it thinks it knows best for society. Ask for the
  threshold and its derivation; there isn't one. Self-defence answers an
  aggressor, not a technology. See [[The Alien Ultimatum - Why Parasitic
  Ethics Cannot Answer Their Own Hypotheticals]]: not even a penny to save
  humanity.
- **Rung:** Authority → Logic. Vault: [[Threats and Risk]].
- **Example:** "ban superintelligence until it's proven safe"; "license
  models because of bioweapons."

## Credentials over argument
- **Shape:** the speaker's authority stands in for the argument. Moe Boy's
  tactic #4.
- **Answer:** the argument stands or falls on its own; so does ours.
- **Rung:** Authority.

## Our own side's weak patterns

The red team checks for these, and each one gets resolved (fixed, verified,
or cut) before a brief is written. Never rest a rebuttal on them. They're about
being *wrong*, not about being unpopular: an argument the general public won't
like is not on this list.

- **Capability forecast:** "AI can't write X", "this tech will never Y". A
  claim about what a technology will be able to do is a prediction and can be
  overtaken next quarter. Argue from structure instead. Not to be confused with
  a priori claims from praxeology: "only acting beings value", "the ends are
  human", "production serves consumers" follow from the axiom of action and are
  not forecasts. State them flatly.
- **Irrelevant or unverified side debate:** pulling in a topic that doesn't
  bear on the claim, or whose specifics couldn't be confirmed. A side debate
  that *does* bear on the claim (IP's illegitimacy in a training-data
  argument, money in a housing argument) is the argument going deeper — use it.
- **Imprecision:** a looser word than the one the other side used. If they
  said "reverse engineering," don't answer "reading code."
- **Unverified specifics:** a number or anecdote that couldn't be confirmed.
  Verify it or cut it.
