# Structure

How a long-form video essay is built, and why each piece is there. Read this
before filling in `01-outline.md`.

## The retention reality

None of what follows is stylistic preference. It exists because of the shape of
the curve:

- Retention starts near 100%, falls sharply through the first 30–60 seconds,
  then declines gradually. **The steepest drop is seconds 10–20**, with the
  inflection around second 15.
- Healthy is **65–80% still watching at 0:30**.
- A typical video retains **~24% at the end**; more than half of all drop-off
  happens inside the first minute.
- For 15–30 minute long-form, **~35% average view duration is strong** — about
  seven minutes of a twenty-minute video.

The consequence worth internalising: a well-paced twenty-minute essay with good
retention beats a shorter video with the same absolute watch time. Length is not
the enemy. Slack is.

The other consequence: **when retention is bad, the fix is earlier in the
script, never later in the edit.** You cannot cut your way out of an opening
that didn't earn attention.

## The first thirty seconds, beat by beat

Scripts that deliver a specific value claim within 15 seconds average about
**52% retention against 44%** for those that don't — an eight-point swing off
one structural decision.

**0–5s · Pattern interrupt.** A bold claim or an unexpected image. No greeting.
No "in this video." No channel branding. No throat-clearing of any kind. The
viewer arrived mid-thought and should feel it.

**5–15s · Payoff promise.** Specifically what they get. "You'll understand why
every attempt at this fails for the same reason" beats "let's talk about this."
Specific — and deliverable, which the audit checks.

**15–30s · Commitment hook.** The information gap that makes leaving feel like
missing something. The strongest version is a question the cold open raised and
hasn't answered.

For this vault's material the hook pattern is **contrarian + curiosity gap**: a
compressed restatement of the argument's most counterintuitive claim. Never a
topic announcement. The essays already contain the hook — it's usually the line
that made the essay worth writing, currently sitting in paragraph four.

## Chapters

Each chapter is **one claim doing one job**. Not one topic — one claim. A
chapter that doesn't advance the thesis gets cut, not shortened.

Chapter length is set by the claim, not by a target. But two constraints bite:

- YouTube requires **at least 10 seconds** per chapter — the build tool enforces
  this, along with the first-stamp-is-00:00 and minimum-three rules.
- A chapter running past ~4 minutes usually contains two claims. Split it.

Name chapters as claims or questions, not labels. "The Calculation Problem" is a
label. "Nobody Can Know What The Price Knows" is a chapter title someone scrubs
back to.

## Open loops

**One every 2–3 minutes.** A loop is a question raised and deliberately left
open. Each resolution should open the next one — a chapter that resolves
cleanly and opens nothing is exactly where viewers leave, because you just told
them the story is over.

The cold open is itself an unresolved loop. The close is its callback. Track
them in the outline table so you can see at a glance where the gaps are — a
four-minute stretch with no open loop is a hole.

## Pattern interrupts

**Every 60–90 seconds**: a visual, audio, or content change. For faceless
narration that means a new graphic, a hard cut to black, a chart replacing text,
a shift in music, or a beat of silence.

Mark them in the outline before scripting. Interrupts added afterwards are
decoration; interrupts planned in advance land on the actual seams of the
argument, which is where they do work.

## The mid-point

Put a deliberate retention hook around the 50% mark — the strongest single
piece of evidence, the most vivid comparison, the moment the argument turns.
Whatever the video's best asset is, it does not belong at the end where fewer
people see it.

## The close

The close is the loop's payoff, not a summary. Return to the cold open's image
with the meaning the viewer now has. Then the affirmative case, stated plainly
and briefly.

Resist the summary-of-what-we-covered ending. It signals "you can stop now"
several seconds before the video actually ends, and it teaches viewers that
your endings are skippable.

## Sanity checks before scripting

- Can you state the thesis in one sentence? If not, it's two videos.
- Does the argument spine hold as a bare syllogism with all rhetoric removed?
- Is there a loop open at every point in the runtime?
- Does the hook promise something the body actually delivers?
- Is the best material in the first 30 seconds and the middle, rather than saved
  for an ending most people won't reach?
