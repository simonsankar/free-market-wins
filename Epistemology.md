**Epistemology is the theory of knowledge — the study of how man validly comes to know what is real.**

## What It Answers

[[Metaphysics]] establishes that reality exists independent of consciousness. Epistemology asks the next question: given that, how does a consciousness — specifically a *human* consciousness — actually come to know it, and how can it tell knowledge apart from error?

## Reason as the Only Means of Knowledge

Man has no automatic, infallible grasp of reality. Perception hands him raw data; everything past that — every concept, every generalization, every piece of knowledge beyond what's immediately perceived — has to be built by a *volitional* process: reason. Reason identifies and integrates what the senses provide, and it is man's only means of knowledge. There is no alternative source — not revelation, not intuition, not consensus, not feeling — that can hand a consciousness a fact about reality without going through this process.

## The Method: Non-Contradictory Identification

Because [[Identity]] holds — a thing cannot be both what it is and not what it is — valid thought is bound by the [[Law of Non-Contradiction]]. Two claims that contradict cannot both be true; if a conclusion produces a contradiction, something upstream in the reasoning was wrong. This isn't a preference for tidiness — it's the only method of thought that can actually track a reality that has a fixed identity.

## Objectivity: Neither Intrinsic Nor Subjective

Knowledge is **objective** when it's the product of reason operating on the facts of reality — neither:
- **Intrinsic** — "just given," supposedly self-evident with no process of thought required, nor
- **Subjective** — whatever a consciousness feels or wishes, unconstrained by reality.

This distinction matters past epistemology proper. See [[Philosophy]] for why conflating "objective" with "administered by a single monopoly authority" is exactly the mistake that corrupts a popular account of this chain's political conclusions.

## Feeds Into

Because man survives by reason rather than instinct, and reason is volitional — he must *choose* to think — man needs the freedom to exercise that faculty and act on its verdicts. That's the epistemological root of [[Ethics]]: a being who survives by a fallible, chosen process of thought needs principles for how to think and act well, applied by himself to himself, not handed down by someone else's claimed certainty. See [[Philosophy]].
