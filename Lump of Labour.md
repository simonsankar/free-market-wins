title: Lump of Labour

The lump-of-labour fallacy is the belief that there's a fixed amount of work to go around, so anything that does work — a machine, an immigrant, a longer working week, a cheaper import — takes it from someone else. The term goes back to the economist David Schloss in 1891, and the belief is far older.

## Why It's Wrong

Work isn't a pie. It's whatever people are willing to pay to have done, and what they want done has no ceiling. Human wants are unlimited; the means to satisfy them are [[Scarcity|scarce]]. Work exists because of that gap, and the gap never closes.

So when something makes one kind of work cheaper, the wants don't go away. The resources freed up go to the next want in line — the one that couldn't be afforded before. Scarcity doesn't disappear; it moves to the next bottleneck.

The historical record is unambiguous. Around 1900, roughly 40% of American workers farmed; today it's under 2%. The other 38 percentage points weren't left idle. They went into factories, offices, hospitals, and jobs that didn't exist in 1900, because once food was cheap, people wanted other things. The same happened with weavers and power looms, typists and word processors, switchboard operators and automatic exchanges, human "computers" and electronic ones.

## The Shape of the Argument

The fallacy always runs the same way:

1. One input gets cheap or powerful.
2. Assume it was the thing holding everything up.
3. Assume nobody responds — no new products, no new wants, no new work.
4. Conclude collapse.
5. Hand the fix to the government: bans, quotas, shorter weeks, job guarantees.

Step 3 is the error. It requires people *not* to act on changed conditions, which is to deny [[Human action]] itself. People always respond, because they always have unmet wants.

## The Strong Version

The modern form says: *this time the machine substitutes for cognition itself, so there's no higher rung to climb to.* History of automated layers doesn't answer that by itself. What does:

- **The ends are human.** Production exists to serve human wants. Only acting beings [[Subjective Value Theory|value]] anything, so a machine that does every task still does it *for* someone — and that someone is the consumer who gains when goods get cheap.
- **Humans can own the machines.** The horse, displaced by the tractor, couldn't buy a tractor. People can own the capital that replaces their labour, and cheaper goods raise real income even where wages fall.
- **Some wants are human by definition** — trust, accountability, presence, things made by a person. That isn't a forecast about technology; it's a statement about what people value.

Where ownership is concentrated and housing and land keep getting more expensive, the cause isn't the machine. It's money that can't hold value, pushing savings into hard assets and handing new money to asset owners first ([[Fiat Currency]], [[Cantillon Effect]]).
