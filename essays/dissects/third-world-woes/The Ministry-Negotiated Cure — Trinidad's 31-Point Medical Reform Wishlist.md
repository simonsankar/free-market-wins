## A 31-Point Plan to Nationalize Trinidadian Healthcare, One Ministry Negotiation at a Time

**The Stated Problem**

An r/trinidad "Questions/Discussions" post lays out 31 "doable steps" for Trinidad's healthcare system: constitutionalize a right to both public and private health, adopt a Universal Health System modeled on France, negotiate all medical and pharmacy fees between the Ministry of Health and physicians every two years, mandate 30-minute maximum wait times under threat of automatic suspension, dissolve the Medical Board in favor of a foreign-led disciplinary body, tax sugar/fat/salt while subsidizing "approved" healthy foods by 30%, and — long term — build state nursing homes, state medical schools, state factories for local medicine production, and a state medical research institute.

**The Shallow Take**

The diagnosis throughout is *insufficient state coverage, insufficient state coordination, insufficient state-negotiated pricing*. Every failure mode Trinidad's healthcare system currently exhibits — the stillbirth at San Fernando General, hours-long waits, understaffing — gets the same prescription: more centralized control, administered better this time, backed by "STRICTLY regulated" fees and imported foreign disciplinarians to keep the professionals in line. Not one of the 31 points asks whether a state monopsony buyer setting prices for an entire medical sector is the mechanism producing the queues and negligence in the first place. It only asks whether the ministry is negotiating hard enough.

**The Actual Sin(s)**

**1. NAP Violation — Every Line Item Is Someone Else's Wallet**

"State covers," "state borne," "free," "government subsidized 30%," "wholly free medical school" — this vocabulary recurs in nearly every point. None of it is free. All of it routes through [[Taxation]]: coerced extraction from productive Trinidadians to fund $100 doctor visits, $20 pharmacy runs, free nursing homes with "internet and dietician," free medical school with debt forgiveness, and food delivery for the ill. The word "free" here does the same work [[Taxation]] describes it doing everywhere else — hiding "seized from someone who never agreed to fund it" behind a fiscal illusion. A right to healthcare, as proposed in point 1, is not a right at all under [[Argumentation Ethics]] — a genuine right cannot impose a positive obligation of labor on a doctor, pharmacist, or taxpayer. It's a claim on other people's bodies and property dressed up in constitutional language.

**2. Economic Calculation Problem — A Price-Fixing Cartel of One Buyer**

Point 3's "Cost management" is the plan's structural core: government negotiates fees for *every* medical service and medicine nationally, "STRICTLY regulated," fixed every two years, with the explicit goal that "no one place or doctor will charge different from another." This is not cost containment — it is the abolition of the price mechanism in medicine. Per the [[Economic Calculation Problem]], once the state becomes the near-exclusive payer setting a single uniform price across the entire sector, there is no longer a market price signaling which procedures are scarce, which specialists are in genuine short supply, or where capital should flow. The ministry is "groping in the dark" with your body's care the same way Mises showed socialist planners grope in the dark with capital goods. A single negotiated price cannot simultaneously reflect the cost structure of a rural GP and a specialist surgeon in Port of Spain — uniform pricing guarantees shortages in some services and gluts in others, discovered only after the damage is done.

**3. Knowledge Problem — 30 Minutes, Decreed From a Ministry Office**

Point 16 mandates a hard 30-minute wait cap "under pain of automatic suspension." This assumes a central rule can substitute for the dispersed, real-time information a doctor and patient generate about triage priority, case complexity, and emergency load on a given day. The [[Knowledge Problem (Information Throughput Problem)]] shows why this fails structurally: no fixed rule set by 10 or 100 officials can process what millions of individual doctor-patient interactions require moment to moment. The predictable result isn't faster care — it's defensive medicine, rushed diagnoses to beat the clock, and doctors declining complex cases that risk running long, exactly the failure mode already killing patients at San Fernando General.

**4. Incentive Inversion — Suspending the Doctors, Not the Ministry**

The plan dissolves the Medical Board (point 17) and imports foreign disciplinarians to punish doctors for rudeness, slowness, and malpractice — while the Ministry of Health, the entity actually setting the fee schedule, staffing levels, and facility budgets, faces zero equivalent accountability. This is the [[Inverse incentive structure]] in its purest form: the party with a profit-and-loss discipline (private doctors) gets threatened with suspension for symptoms; the party immune to profit and loss (the ministry setting the conditions that produce those symptoms) gets a bigger mandate — a mental health ministry, more hospitals, more nursing homes, more research institutes. When a system fails under central planning, the answer is never "less central planning," it's "central planning, but angrier this time."

**5. Consent Fiction and the France Citation That Undercuts Itself**

Point 3 explicitly cites France's "number one ranked" system as the model — the same negotiated-tariff mechanism (the *convention médicale*) that this plan proposes replicating. That mechanism is precisely what has produced France's *déserts médicaux* (medical deserts) and repeated GP strikes over ministry-set fees in recent years: doctors refusing to practice, or leaving regions entirely, because price controls made practicing there unprofitable. The plan holds up as its model a system whose central pricing mechanism is actively driving out the doctors it depends on. This is not a hypothetical failure mode — it is the observed present-tense outcome of the exact policy being proposed.

**6. The GATE-Bond Trap, Recompiled for Medicine**

Point 7's free medical school with debt cleared is the same structure already dissected in [[The Cradle-to-PhD Central Plan — Trinidad's Education Reform Wishlist]]: state-funded credentials create an implicit (or eventually explicit) obligation to serve locally at ministry-negotiated rates, precisely while those doctors' market value is highest. Combined with point 6's "little to no need to go abroad" framing, this reads as a pipeline designed to produce doctors who trained for free and now face a captive local market where the only buyer sets the price. That's not a healthcare workforce strategy — it's an indenture with extra steps, and it's the exact mechanism that fuels the brain drain [[Inverse incentive structure]] already describes.

**7. Cantillon Effect — Picking Winners in the Grocery Aisle**

Point 14 subsidizes "approved" healthy foods 30% — "including foreign brands" — while point 13 taxes sugar, fat, and salt. Per the [[Cantillon Effect]], this newly directed spending doesn't reach consumers evenly: it flows first to the producers and importers whose products the ministry has pre-approved (Glucerna, specific chocolate brands, Splenda), who benefit from guaranteed demand at subsidized prices before market signals ever determined whether Trinidadians actually wanted those products at that price. Meanwhile a tax bureaucracy has to be built to police what counts as "high sugar" versus "healthy," a second central-planning problem stacked on the first.

**8. Monopoly Protection — Wall-to-Wall State Control of an Entire Industry**

State-negotiated fees, state medical schools, state-run nursing homes, state-built hospitals, state medicine factories, state research institutes: by the plan's long-term section, the government is simultaneously the trainer, employer, price-setter, landlord, and regulator of Trinidadian medicine. [[The Myth of Limited Government]] applies directly — a ministry with this scope will not spontaneously constrain itself to the 31 points listed. Every state health system that started as "just" a payer has metastasized into a full-spectrum monopoly, because that is the predictable trajectory of any institution granted this authority, not an accident of poor implementation.

**Market Proof**

The parts of medicine still closest to a free market in the countries this plan cites as models are exactly where prices have fallen and quality has risen — mirroring the iPhone dynamic, not the housing one. LASIK surgery in the U.S., a procedure insurance and government payers largely don't touch, has dropped in real price for decades while outcomes improved, because patients pay cash, providers compete on price and quality, and there's no ministry negotiating a uniform national tariff. Direct primary care clinics — cash-pay, $50–100/month memberships, no insurance middleman — routinely deliver same-day appointments with 30+ minutes of actual doctor time, solving the exact "30 minutes tops" problem this plan tries to legislate into existence with suspension threats, using nothing but price signals and voluntary exchange. Meanwhile, the UK's NHS — a direct architectural cousin of what's being proposed — has a waiting list exceeding 7 million people for elective procedures, and Canada's single-payer system produces some of the longest specialist wait times in the developed world. Trinidad doesn't need a Ministry of Health sophisticated enough to negotiate fees for an entire nation's medicine biannually. It needs the barriers — licensing cartels, price controls, state monopsony purchasing — removed so that Trinidadian doctors and patients can do what LASIK surgeons and direct-primary-care clinics already do without a single subsidized neighborhood or "toothy" foreign disciplinary board.

**The Verdict**

None of these 31 points fail because Trinidad hasn't found the right foreign consultants to import, or because the fee-negotiation schedule needs to run more often than every two years. They fail because the entire plan rests on the premise that a ministry can calculate the correct price for every medical service and medicine in the country, predict doctor and patient behavior under a 30-minute mandate, and allocate hospital construction and specialist training better than millions of individual transactions between doctors and patients would. The [[Economic Calculation Problem]] and [[Knowledge Problem (Information Throughput Problem)]] independently prove this isn't a competence problem — it's a logical impossibility, regardless of who runs the ministry or how foreign and independent its disciplinary board is. Underneath that sits the deeper violation the whole document never confronts: none of this is fundable without first taking, by force, from people who never agreed to pay for someone else's $0 pharmacy visit, subsidized Glucerna, or state-built nursing home.

The actual path to fixing what happened at San Fernando General isn't a bigger, more tightly price-controlled ministry — it's removing the licensing cartels and price ceilings that already suppress the supply of doctors and facilities, and letting patients and providers negotiate directly the way LASIK clinics and direct primary care already prove works. Trinidad doesn't need a healthcare system copied from France's failing *convention médicale*. It needs fewer obstacles between sick people and the doctors willing to treat them for a price both sides agree to.
