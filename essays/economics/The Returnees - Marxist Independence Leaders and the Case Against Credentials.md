## The pattern

A striking number of 20th-century independence movements were led by one man who left the colony, studied in the metropole (or Moscow), absorbed Marxism or a close cousin of it, came home, won independence, and then ran the new state on the doctrine he had been taught. The independence was real. The economics that followed was a transplant.

Not all of them were strict Marxists, and the argument doesn't need them to be. What they share is the **collectivist frame**: the nation as one body for the state to direct, instead of millions of individuals who own things and trade. That frame descends from Marx whether or not the man carried a party card.

The claim of this essay: **a degree is not economic understanding.** What the returnees got abroad was a worldview, delivered by a curriculum and a social circle that had already decided the answer. They never learned how prices, property and calculation work, so they never saw the problem with replacing them. That is [[Central planning apologists]] at national scale, with a flag attached.

## The roll call

"All" is a strong word, because nobody maintains an official list and the criteria are contestable. My criteria: the **main figure** of the independence movement, who **studied abroad** and held a **Marxist or Marxist-inspired** ideology. I split them by how cleanly they fit.

### Tier 1 - Studied abroad, avowed Marxist, led the country to independence

| Leader | Country (independence) | Where they studied | Ideology | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kwame Nkrumah | Ghana (1957) | Lincoln University (Pennsylvania), UPenn, briefly LSE | Called himself a "non-denominational Christian and a Marxist socialist"; "Consciencism" | State enterprises, forced saving from cocoa farmers, debt and burned reserves; overthrown 1966 |
| Agostinho Neto | Angola (1975) | Medicine at Coimbra and Lisbon; Casa dos Estudantes do Império circle | MPLA openly Marxist-Leninist, Soviet and Cuban backed | One-party state, civil war; Marxism-Leninism dropped around 1990 |
| Manuel Pinto da Costa | São Tomé and Príncipe (1975) | Economics degree **and doctorate** at East Berlin | MLSTP, Marxist-Leninist | Tried to build socialism, shifted to Western aid from about 1985; multiparty politics in 1990, MLSTP lost in 1991 |
| Robert Mugabe | Zimbabwe (1980) | Fort Hare (South Africa); London and UNISA external degrees, taught in Ghana | Self-described Marxist-Leninist in the 1970s | Breadbasket to hyperinflation |
| Ho Chi Minh | Vietnam (1945, secured 1954) | Lived in Paris, founding member of the French Communist Party; Communist University of the Toilers of the East, Moscow | Marxist-Leninist | Command economy; reversed by Đổi Mới in 1986 |

Pinto da Costa is the cleanest case. His *economics* training was itself East German Marxist economics. He was credentialed in exactly the field he then wrecked.

### Tier 2 - Studied abroad, Marxist-inspired, but the fit is imperfect

| Leader | Country | Studied | Why it's imperfect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amílcar Cabral | Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde | Agronomy, Lisbon | Used Marxist analysis but refused the label. **Assassinated in 1973**, before independence |
| Eduardo Mondlane | Mozambique | Oberlin, Northwestern (PhD) | Founded FRELIMO, socialist-leaning. **Assassinated in 1969.** Samora Machel was the independence-day leader and had no foreign university |
| Forbes Burnham | Guyana (1966) | University of London (BA, LLB), Gray's Inn | A socialist at independence. Later said "there is only one kind of socialism... based on Marxism-Leninism", declared a "Cooperative Republic" in 1970 and nationalized bauxite and sugar in 1971-76 |
| Modibo Keïta | Mali (1960) | École William Ponty, Dakar (a different colony of the same empire) | Joined a communist study group; one-party state, extensive nationalization |
| Souphanouvong | Laos | Civil engineering, Paris | Founded the Pathet Lao; joined the Indochinese Communist Party in 1950. Independence came earlier under a royal government, his power in 1975 |

### Tier 3 - Same schools, same mistake, not technically Marxist

### The recruiters

Two people did the pipeline's logistics, and one is from home. **George Padmore** (Trinidad) ran the Comintern's Negro Workers committee in Moscow (1929 onward), later advised Nkrumah in Ghana, and was a contact for Kenyatta. **Cheddi Jagan** (Guyana) studied at Howard and Northwestern, was a Marxist-Leninist, and lost the independence race to Burnham.

### The pipeline

- **Paris:** Ho Chi Minh, Zhou Enlai and Deng Xiaoping (China, semi-colonial), Souphanouvong, Senghor, and the Khmer Rouge leadership.
- **London (LSE, Fabians, the Communist Party):** Nkrumah, Kenyatta, Burnham, Lee Kuan Yew.
- **Lisbon (Casa dos Estudantes do Império):** Cabral, Neto, Mário Pinto de Andrade, Marcelino dos Santos. The regime meant it to train colonial administrators. It produced the men who fought Portugal.
- **Moscow (KUTV, then Lumumba University from 1960):** Ho, Kenyatta, and later generations.
- **East Berlin:** Pinto da Costa.
- **American Black colleges:** Nkrumah (Lincoln), Jagan (Howard).

## The control group: the educated who weren't indoctrinated

If foreign study alone caused this, it would be universal. It isn't, and the exceptions matter.

- **Jomo Kenyatta (Kenya):** studied in Moscow in 1932-33 and at LSE, then ran a market-oriented Kenya. Next door, Nyerere ran Tanzania on Ujamaa.
- **Lee Kuan Yew (Singapore):** Cambridge, a Fabian phase, a "marriage of convenience" with communists. He then dropped the doctrine because it didn't work. Singapore is the result.
- **Seretse Khama (Botswana):** Oxford. Protected property and kept the diamonds under a rule-bound state.
- **Houphouët-Boigny (Ivory Coast):** his RDA party was allied with the French Communists until about 1950, then he broke with them. Ivory Coast boomed until the 1980s.
- **Hastings Banda (Malawi):** studied in the US and Edinburgh, was fiercely anti-communist, and still ran a poor dictatorship. Not everything is Marx. Banda is the honest limit of the thesis.

So the **specific curriculum and company** mattered, not "education" in the abstract. And the ones who escaped did so through **contact with reality**, not through more schooling.

## Case study: Eric Williams and the book that explains the man

Williams is the Trinidad case, and his doctoral thesis, published as *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), shows the collectivist frame in its purest form. It makes two claims. First, that profits from the slave trade and the Caribbean plantations financed the British Industrial Revolution. Second, the "decline thesis": that the slave economy was already in economic decline, so abolition followed economics rather than morals.

**The libertarian objection is to the title itself.** Capitalism is the institutionalized respect for self-ownership and voluntary exchange. Slavery is the abolition of both, with the owner of the person defined as property. They are opposites, so a book that fuses them has misdefined its central term before it starts. It treats everything that happened in the economy of the period as one block labeled "capitalism" and then convicts the block.

What actually sustained Caribbean slavery was the state:

- **Mercantilist monopoly:** the Navigation Acts and the sugar preferences protected West Indian planters from competition. That is state privilege, not the market.
- **The bailout:** when Britain abolished slavery in 1833 it paid about £20 million **to the slave owners**, not the enslaved. The Treasury itself has described this as about 40% of the national budget, and the debt was only finished off in 2015, paid by ordinary taxpayers. (Figures for the modern equivalent vary widely by source.) A system that needs the state to underwrite it, and then to pay it off when it ends, was never a free market.
- **Plunder isn't production.** Even if slave profits were large, taking what others made by force transfers wealth and misallocates capital. It doesn't create it. The [[Seen and Unseen]] applies: the planters' profit is seen, the lost output of the people they owned is not.

Two corrections to keep this honest:

- Williams did not ignore the Industrial Revolution. He wrote about it at length. His error was in crediting plunder as its financier, over savings, invention and entrepreneurship. That claim has been contested since it was published. Drescher's *Econocide* and the Engerman school of economists (including Roger Anstey's 1968 critique) attack both halves of the thesis, and the decline thesis has taken the heaviest damage. The thesis still has academic defenders, so I'm not claiming the debate is closed. I'm claiming it is contested and, on the main point, the critics have the better case.
- Williams opposed slavery. The "leaning toward the other direction" argument is the libertarian one: a worldview that sees the economy as one extractive block ends up wanting the state to take the block. That is Hayek's road to serfdom. It is not literal chattel slavery. Williams ran Trinidad as head of government for 25 years, 1956 until his death in 1981, under a "pragmatic socialism" that tied development to state direction and foreign capital. For what the Trinidadian state looks like today, see [[Trinidad — Mentality, Monopoly, and the Machinery of a Third-World State]].

Notice also what the book does for its author. If capitalism built the West on stolen labor, then the colony is owed, and the one who collects on its behalf is the man who holds the state. The theory of grievance doubles as a claim to power.

## The mechanism: primacy of consciousness at national scale

Each returnee made the same error, [[Primacy of Consciousness]]: *if the right people with the right idea hold the state, the economy will do what they intend.* Nkrumah's slogan was "seek ye first the political kingdom." Politics first, production later, as if wealth were a decision.

Three things followed from that.

1. **They never faced the [[Economic Calculation Problem]].** Abolish private ownership of capital goods and there are no prices for them, so no way to know whether a steel mill beats a cattle ranch. [[Socialism is impossible]] regardless of the planner's sincerity, or his degree.
2. **They inherited the colonial state's tools and kept them.** The colonial marketing boards extracted from peasant farmers. Independence leaders kept the boards and spent the proceeds on prestige industrialization (Robert Bates documented this for tropical Africa). The ideology blessed it as "development."
3. **The doctrine suited the man holding the state.** Marxism-Leninism hands the leader a theory for why the colony's resources belong to the state, and therefore to him. See [[State]], [[Regulatory Capture]]. It is the one worldview in which the intellectual's own power is also the cure. The incentives and the ideas pointed the same way, which is why it spread so well.

The mainstream development economics of the 1950s and 60s also leaned toward planning and import substitution. Plenty of non-Marxist advisers told these men what they wanted to hear. The Marxists were the enthusiastic end of a broad consensus.

## Reality's verdict

Many of these states reversed course, in the same years and in the same direction:

- **Vietnam:** Đổi Mới, 1986.
- **Tanzania:** Nyerere stepped down in 1985; Mwinyi liberalized.
- **Ghana:** the 1983 recovery program, after the state enterprises were shut or sold.
- **São Tomé:** a turn to Western aid from about 1985, multiparty politics in 1990.
- **Angola:** dropped Marxism-Leninism around 1990.
- **India:** the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis forced liberalization.

One comparison. Around 1957, Ghana and South Korea had broadly comparable per-capita income by commonly cited estimates (sources vary on who was slightly ahead). By 2023 the gap was roughly fourteen-fold in nominal terms. Korea was no laissez-faire state, so I'm not claiming it was a clean market case. But it never abolished property, which is the comparison that matters.

## The American founders, honestly

| | American founders | Marxist returnees |
|---|---|---|
| **Education** | Mostly domestic colleges (William & Mary, Harvard, Princeton, King's College); a few read law at the Inns of Court | Metropolitan and Soviet-bloc institutions with a political curriculum |
| **Intellectual diet** | Locke, Montesquieu, Blackstone, Hume, and *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) | Marx, Lenin, Fabians, Comintern |
| **Economic experience** | Planters, merchants, printers, lawyers: people with property and trade at stake | Teachers, lawyers, doctors, journalists, activists: mostly no business experience |
| **Self-government before independence** | About 150 years of colonial assemblies | Little or none |
| **What the revolt was about** | A revolt against taxes and trade restrictions, aimed at limiting the state | A revolt for ownership of the colony, aimed at capturing the state |
| **First act with power** | A constitution of enumerated powers | A one-party state |

Be honest about the limits. The founders were not free-market purists. Hamilton's bank, tariffs and debt assumption were mercantilist, and slavery makes any "pure liberty" framing false. But they argued about *how much* state, from a tradition that distrusted state power. The returnees argued about *who* should hold it, from a tradition that sanctified it.

The relevant difference isn't that one group went to school and the other didn't. One group's economic understanding came from **skin in the game** ([[Skin in the game]]): they held property, they traded, they bore losses. The other's came from a syllabus. A syllabus can't be falsified by a lost harvest, so it never needs to update until the whole country pays for it.

## How big is the pattern?

The roll call shows the pattern exists. It doesn't show how common it is. Counting honestly:

- **Statism was close to universal, socialism was not.** One tally says 35 of 53 African regimes called themselves socialist at some point between 1960 and 1980. That is a loose measure, because it counts declarations rather than sustained policy. The Marxist-Leninist wave was much smaller and later, peaking from the mid-1970s to the mid-1980s.
- **Among foreign-educated first leaders, it's roughly an even split.** A rough hand-count from memory, not a dataset. On the state-led side: Nkrumah, Nyerere, Neto, Mugabe, Pinto da Costa, Cabral, Nehru, Burnham. On the market-leaning side: Kenyatta, Banda, Khama, Lee Kuan Yew, Ramgoolam (Mauritius), Olympio (Togo). That is not a vast majority.
- **Foreign study was not required.** Touré, Machel, Kaunda and Aristides Pereira (Cape Verde) ran socialist states without a foreign university.
- **What does line up is militancy.** The most confrontational anti-colonial movements, the ones that organized mass parties or fought wars, skew heavily toward the left. Several of the leaders who inherited power through negotiation with the colonial power, such as Houphouët-Boigny and Kenyatta, went the other way. A proper test needs a leader-education dataset, and I haven't found one that codes economic policy.
- **The research on foreign education points the other direction on politics.** [Spilimbergo](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/democracy-and-foreign-education-20082) finds that foreign-educated people promote democracy at home, but only if they studied in democracies. [Gift and Krcmaric](https://metatoc.com/papers/61526-who-democratizes-western-educated-leaders-and-regime-transitions) find Western-educated leaders improve democratization prospects. I found nothing on economic liberalization specifically.
- **Foreign education transmits whatever school dominates.** The mirror image is the Chicago-trained economists who liberalized Chile, and Manmohan Singh, Cambridge and Oxford educated, who opened India in 1991. The 1930s-50s metropole had a left-leaning intellectual scene. That is what the returnees carried home.

## The easy pass

"Everyone believed in planning back then" doesn't excuse them, because the alternative was available, in the same decade, to the same kind of man:

- **Singapore** took the advice of the Dutch economist Albert Winsemius, who led a UN mission in 1960 and then served as chief economic adviser until the 1980s. His advice was to attract foreign investment for export industries, which was against conventional thinking at the time. [Lee wrote that he learned much from him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Winsemius). This is the "hire the people who know" option.
- **Hong Kong**, a colony, ran on John Cowperthwaite's ["positive non-interventionism"](https://fee.org/articles/the-man-behind-the-hong-kong-miracle/) from 1961 to 1971. He refused to collect detailed economic statistics because they invite meddling.
- **The ideas existed.** Mises published the calculation argument in 1920. Hayek's *Road to Serfdom* came out in 1944. Erhard liberalized West Germany in 1948.

The returnees were not ignorant of the alternative. They were in the same libraries and in the same cities as the people who built it.

## What happened afterward, honestly

The claim that life was worse than under the colonial powers needs care. Sub-Saharan life expectancy [rose from about 40 in 1960 to about 51 in 1993](https://www.aph.gov.au/~/media/wopapub/house/committee/jfadt/africa/Aftbl8_1_pdf.ashx), though it lagged other regions. Income is the problem. [Per-capita growth averaged about 1.5% in the 1960s, 0.8% in the 1970s and -1.2% in the 1980s](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/1998/06/1998b_bpea_bloom_sachs_collier_udry.pdf). That is a fade and then a collapse, and it tracks the years the state's grip tightened. Oil shocks, debt and wars contributed, so this is consistent with the thesis, not proof of it. Some cases do match "worse than before": Ghana in the 1980s, Angola and Mozambique in their wars, and Zimbabwe in 2008.

The new states also kept the extractive machinery of the colonial state and pointed it at their own people. Colonialism ended and the colonial apparatus didn't.

## Where the thesis is too strong

- **Selection.** The roll call counts the cases that fit. The fair test is the whole denominator of independence leaders, and the control group above shows it isn't uniform.
- **"Education is indoctrination" overshoots.** Education was a transmission channel. Unions and parties carried the doctrine too: Sékou Touré (Guinea) built his Marxism through the labor movement, and Sukarno developed his syncretism at home. And the best critics of the planning consensus, such as Mises, Hayek and Peter Bauer, were also educated abroad.
- **Cold War money** funded the scholarships and the movements. Part of the pipeline was supply, not demand.

The defensible version: **credentials certify exposure, not understanding, and an economy run on a doctrine its leader never had to test in business is a hostage to that doctrine.**

Where sources conflict, I say so. Mugabe's exact degrees, Kenyatta's Moscow institution, Nkrumah's degrees and Mondlane's PhD institution all vary by source. The Ghana-Korea 1957 figures come from popular secondary sources and should be checked against Maddison or World Bank historical series before being quoted. The Eric Williams section draws on [Britannica and other biographies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Williams), a [summary of the Williams thesis and its critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery), and reporting on the [1833 compensation and its 2015 repayment](https://www.aaihs.org/british-culpability-in-the-trading-and-enslavement-of-africans/). The compensation figure is the Treasury's own "40% of the national budget" claim, and sources disagree on the denominator and the modern equivalent. The Navigation Acts and sugar-preference point is from general knowledge. Claims about Houphouët-Boigny, Seretse Khama, Banda, Nyerere's Edinburgh degree, Hatta, the Hindu rate of growth, Angola's dropping Marxism-Leninism, and the marketing-board literature (Bates, Bauer) come from general knowledge and were not re-verified in this session.
