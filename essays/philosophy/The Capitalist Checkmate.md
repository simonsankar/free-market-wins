## One ethic wearing many costumes

Strip away the branding and there are only two political ethics. Everything else is implementation detail.

The first is the [[The Non-aggression Principle|universal ethic]]: no one may initiate force against another's person or property. It applies to everyone, equally, without exception carved out for a class, a party, or a percentage of the vote.

The second is the aggression ethic: it is permissible to take from some people and give to others, provided the taking is dressed up correctly. Democratic socialism, monarchism, full communism, technocratic central planning — these are not different ethics. They are the same ethic wearing different uniforms. The vote count, the bloodline, the five-year plan: none of that is the principle, it's just the *mechanism* by which the principle gets enforced. The principle underneath all of them is identical — some people's claims on property and labor outrank other people's.

That's [[Polylogism]] with better PR. It requires a silent premise that humanity isn't actually one category — that the person being taxed and the person being subsidized don't operate under the same rules, that one is a means and the other an end. A universal ethic can't do that. Only a tribal one can. So when someone tells you their preferred system is just "regulated capitalism" or "democracy with a safety net," ask what the underlying principle is, not what the mechanism is called. If the answer requires taking from A without A's consent to give to B, the mechanism is cosmetic. It's [[Aggression]] in a nicer font.

## Two kinds of opponents

Once you sort people by ethic instead of by party label, you find they split into two very different groups — and only one of them is worth arguing with.

**The lost cause.** This person will not grant the premises of logic itself when the premises are inconvenient. Point out that theft can't be the engine of abundance because coercion strictly reduces what gets produced, and they won't refute it — they'll evade it. "That's just semantics." "This has nothing to do with starving people." They aren't defending a position, they're guarding an identity — usually one built on being part of an in-group, an underdog, a righteous *us* against a villainous *them*. You cannot lose an argument you refuse to have. That's not a debate strategy, it's a psychological one, and no amount of [[Argumentation Ethics]] gets through it, because they've already opted out of argumentation as a method for finding truth.

**The good-faith interlocutor.** This person will actually grant the premise. "Yeah, I know it's coercive. I know it punishes production. I just think the outcome is worth it." This is a completely different animal — it's a stated preference, not a claim to logical high ground. It's someone saying "I know this is wrong, and I want it anyway," the same epistemic status as believing in the tooth fairy: mistaken, but not hostile, and not evasive. These people can update. Show them what they're actually paying for — in productivity, in the poor they claim to champion staying poor — and some of them will move. Not all. But they're reachable, because they never left the room where reasoning happens.

The lost cause is who you write off. The good-faith interlocutor is who you argue with. But there's a decisive move available against the lost cause specifically — one that doesn't require them to engage, agree, or ever admit they've lost.

## Why argument alone doesn't end it

Here's the problem with pure argumentation against a lost cause: it has no finish line. They can evade forever. There's no referee, no bell, no scorecard — just an infinite regress of "well actually" that lets someone walk away from a conversation they lost and still tell themselves, and everyone watching, that they won.

Compare that to a fight. Two people who can't settle something with words step into a ring, and when it's over, it's over. Nobody argues about who's still standing. The format itself supplies the finality that argument couldn't. Not because violence settles truth — it doesn't — but because both parties have implicitly agreed to a mechanism where the outcome can't be talked away afterward.

What you want, arguing against a lost cause, is something with that same structural property: a claim they cannot evade, walk back, or reframe after the fact, because it isn't resting on their willingness to engage with logic at all. It has to be a fact about their own behavior, visible to everyone, that argues for you regardless of what they say next.

There is one.

## The checkmate

It doesn't matter whether the belief is broadcast to an audience or just muttered between two friends over drinks about how "the rich" should really be paying more. Nobody needs a platform, a following, or a manifesto for this to apply. Anyone who holds that theft becomes acceptable once the state does the stealing is making the same claim, at whatever volume they happen to make it. Scale doesn't change the ethic, and it doesn't change the checkmate.

Zoom out before getting to the individual, and the pattern already shows itself at civilizational scale: nobody is fleeing *toward* the workers' paradises. Cubans build rafts out of scrap and inner tubes to get off the island; nobody builds one to get on it. Venezuelans have walked across a continent to leave; nobody walks in. Same story for North Korea, Nicaragua, every full-throated attempt at the thing — decades of it, dozens of tries, and the migration line only ever points one direction. That's [[Revealed Preference|revealed preference]] at the largest scale there is, and people who hold the ideology anyway are holding it against a mountain of evidence, not an absence of it.

(One scope note: none of this is aimed at parts of the world that never had capital accumulation to begin with — swaths of Africa, Asia, the Middle East lacking the basic infrastructure of a market economy are a different conversation entirely, and not one this essay is having. The checkmate is aimed at people living inside the wealthiest, most capital-rich civilization markets have ever produced, who've concluded that its wealth is the problem.)

Because that's who's actually being talked about here. Every hour of every day, the person insisting capitalism is the root of the world's problems is using nothing but capitalism to insist it. The laptop, the phone, the OS, the ISP, the Gmail account, the YouTube account, the ChatGPT or Claude subscription, the app they're venting in, the electricity, the delivery service that dropped off dinner — every link in that chain is a voluntary transaction with a business they were never forced to patronize. Nobody frog-marched them into the Apple Store.

This is not the same move as "you use public roads, therefore you're a hypocrite for opposing the state" — that gotcha [[The False Equivalence of Usage - Ancaps, Public Services, and the Consent Principle|fails]], because taxation is compelled and refusal to pay ends in a cage. The state doesn't need your vote of confidence to keep operating; it operates on you regardless. That's restitution, not endorsement, and it's a completely different transaction than what's happening here.

Buying a MacBook is not that. Nobody garnishes your wages for an iPhone. You could build your own computer from parts, run open-source everything, host your rant on a server you own, and say not one cent of this touched a corporation I claim to despise. Nobody does this. They choose the products of the system they say they oppose, over and over, with money they earned and could have spent — or not spent — differently. That's not restitution for theft already committed. That's a fresh purchase, freely made, every single time.

And a voluntary purchase is a vote of confidence, because unlike tax revenue, corporate revenue depends entirely on it. No customers, no company. Every dollar is a chosen, revocable act of support. So the accounting is simple: they had the option to abstain, and every day they decline to take it. That's the whole argument, and it doesn't need them to agree with it to land — it's just a description of what they did today.

From here there are only two ways out, and both of them prove the point:

**Denial.** "I have no other option, I have to use these things." Untrue, and they know it — but even taken at face value, notice what they're not doing: seizing it by force, the way their own ethic says is fine when the target is "the rich." They're paying for it, at listed price, same as any capitalist would. The only thing standing between them and theft is the law they'd otherwise call oppressive. They're behaving like a capitalist not because they've been persuaded, but because being anything else would get them arrested. Take away the threat of jail and ask what they'd actually do — the honest answer indicts the ethic, not the excuse.

**The grifter's shrug.** "So what, I'm rich anyway." This one is even better, because it's not even a denial — it's a confession dressed as a flex. Nobody gets rich off socialism except the state functionaries who administer the taking. Everyone else who's wealthy and still calling themselves a socialist got there some other way: content, product, platform, brand — capitalist means, every time. They're not proof socialism works. They're proof it doesn't, delivered by someone standing on a pile of money they made the only way money like that actually gets made.

Either branch, they've already lost — not because you out-argued them in the moment, but because their own daily conduct is the argument, running in the background whether they ever show up to defend it or not. That's the structural advantage a fight has over a debate: it doesn't need the loser's cooperation to produce a verdict.

And here's the part that actually matters: it doesn't require them to notice. Whether they clock the contradiction or go their whole life without thinking about it once, capitalism keeps running underneath them regardless — building the phone, powering the grid, stocking the shelf, restocking it again tomorrow. It doesn't need their vote of confidence to keep producing, and it'll happily take their money either way. Meanwhile the difference in posture is the whole game: some people save, invest, and build a life on their own account, answering to no one for it. Others want to be provided for by a state they never had to lift a finger to fund, and call the arrangement justice. That second thing isn't an ethic. It's a grown adult asking to be parented, dressed up as a political philosophy. Thanks for the business either way. The rest settles itself.
