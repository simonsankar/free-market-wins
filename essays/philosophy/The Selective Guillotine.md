**"You can't derive an ought from an is" is never where the statist starts. It's where he retreats to once every positive argument for the welfare state has fallen over. And the retreat costs him more than he realizes: the guillotine can't be swung at libertarian ethics without cutting off every "ought" he walked in with, including the one holding the blade.**

## The Descent

Every one of these conversations runs the same course. It opens at the top, with something that sounds like pure compassion: *nobody should go without food, so we need a safety net.* Fine. Who pays for it? *Everyone, through taxes.* And if someone declines? *Then they're made to.* By whom, on whose say-so? *By the government, because the majority voted for it.* Why does a majority's vote license force against a person who never agreed? And here the floor starts giving way. [[Democracy is garbage|Democracy]] turns out to be headcount [[Jungle Law]]. [[Taxation]] turns out to be taking at gunpoint with paperwork attached. The safety net turns out to need exactly the kind of aggression its defender would never tolerate from a private individual.

Each step down is an exit. Any one of them is a perfectly reasonable place to stop and say *fine, you're right*. Refuse it, and the next level has to be dug out: law, then the [[Mixed Law|metaphysical equality of man]] (if all men are equal, class-based law can't be derived; if they aren't, who exactly is sub-human?), then [[Polylogism]], then the [[Law of Non-Contradiction]] itself. At the bottom there are two exits left. One is to concede. The other is to go below bedrock, into the basement, and announce that none of it matters because *you can't derive an ought from an is.* Your ethics is just your preference in a lab coat. Nothing obliges anyone to anything.

Compare that with an ordinary policy fight. *Ban crack, it wrecks people's lives.* Whatever you think of the policy, that conversation bottoms out in one step, at a value both sides already hold: nobody wants their life wrecked. Nobody has to go down to the molecular chemistry of the brain, let alone to the law of identity. Most disagreements end there because people already share the values involved. This one only reaches bedrock because one side turns down every exit on the way down.

And notice what the statist's own "ought" is standing on the whole time. *Earn over $100k, owe the state 30%.* Derived from what? Not from economics, not from equality, not from logic. It has nothing underneath it at all. It's a bare assertion with a tax collector attached. Yet the guillotine only ever gets pointed at the side of the table that has foundations.

It's a strange place to end up after starting from "people shouldn't starve." But it is where the argument ends up, so it's worth knowing exactly why the basement floor doesn't hold either.

## What Hume Actually Said — The Strong Version

Hume's original observation in the *Treatise* was modest. Moral writers glide from "is" to "ought" without explaining the jump, and the jump ought to be explained. The modern version, sometimes called Hume's Law, is sharper and is a claim about logic:

> No argument whose premises are purely descriptive can validly reach a normative conclusion.

The reasoning is the same as why you can't validly conclude "Socrates is tall" from premises that only talk about his mortality. A conclusion can't contain what the premises never mentioned. Put "ought" in the conclusion without an "ought" in the premises, and the argument is invalid.

Grant all of it. It's true as far as it goes. The mistake libertarians make is attacking it head-on with "but that's a norm too!" The Humean has an easy reply: *No, I didn't say you ought not. I said you cannot — validly.* And he's right. "Cannot" here isn't a law of physics. Nothing stops anyone from writing "therefore you ought to..." at the bottom of a page of facts. "Cannot" means *the result won't be valid*, and validity is a plain fact about propositions: it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. "That inference is invalid" is an **is**, just like "no triangle has four sides."

That concession is exactly where the guillotine breaks.

## The Argument

Take the Humean's own claim seriously, as a pure description, and follow it.

**The disarm:**

1. **P1.** Hume's Law, by itself, is a descriptive claim. It reports a fact: a certain kind of inference is invalid.
2. **P2.** A purely descriptive claim, by itself, cannot validly entail a normative conclusion. *(This is Hume's Law, applied to itself.)*
3. **P3.** "Your ethics is unjustified, so you should abandon it" is a normative conclusion.
4. **C1.** Therefore Hume's Law, by itself, cannot entail that you should abandon your ethics. *Its own rule forbids it.*
5. **P4.** To turn "your inference is invalid" into "so drop your conclusion," the skeptic has to add a second premise, an **epistemic norm**: *one ought not believe what is not validly derived.*
6. **P5.** That norm is an "ought." By Hume's Law, no set of descriptive premises can validly produce it. It is underived.
7. **C2.** Therefore the skeptic's rebuttal only works because of an underived, non-physical "ought", which is exactly the kind of thing he claims can't bind anyone.

**The dilemma:**

8. **P6.** Either the skeptic holds that the epistemic norm genuinely binds reasoners, or he doesn't.
9. **P7.** If it binds, then some "oughts" bind everyone even though no "is" produced them. "Your ethics rests on an ought that wasn't derived from an is" stops being an objection, because his objection rests on one too. The question becomes *which* oughts are binding, and that gets settled by argument, not by pointing at the gap.
10. **P8.** If it doesn't bind, then invalidity has no force against anyone. He can't say you're wrong to believe anything: not in the NAP, not in a flat earth, not in anything. "You can't derive an ought from an is" shrinks to "I personally don't feel like accepting your conclusion." That's a statement about his mood, not an objection.
11. **C3.** Either way, the guillotine cannot be used against libertarian ethics. Holding it up takes an ought the skeptic can't account for. Dropping it disarms him.

Note what this does and doesn't prove. It doesn't prove the [[The Non-aggression Principle|NAP]]. It clears the ground. The NAP's positive case comes from [[Argumentation Ethics]], and look at how it works: it takes the same kind of norm the skeptic just leaned on and shows that non-aggression is presupposed right alongside it. The epistemic norm and the NAP are siblings. Both are already in force the moment two people sit down to settle a disagreement by reasons instead of by fists. The skeptic can't keep one and throw away the other. He accepted both when he opened his mouth.

## The Retreat Behind the Retreat

The sharpest Humeans see the dilemma coming and try to step around it: *I'm not telling you to do anything. I'm just noting the gap. Believe what you like.*

Good. Then he's said nothing against you. A man who "just notes" that your argument has a gap, while insisting that noting it carries no implication for what you should believe, has made a remark about the weather. He's welcome to it. But he can't then turn around and call a libertarian's conviction *unjustified*, *irrational*, *dogmatic*, or *wrong*. Every one of those words is normative. He gave them up when he stepped back.

## The Selective Guillotine

Here's what gives the game away. Nobody actually holds the position.

The man who reaches for Hume when you say *taxation is theft* never reaches for Hume when he says *billionaires ought to pay their fair share*, *children ought to have healthcare*, or *nobody ought to go hungry*. He doesn't wave off "you shouldn't torture children" as a mere preference with no rational standing. The blade only comes down on *your* oughts, and only after his own have been defeated on the merits. He is a moral realist all the way through the conversation and a moral nihilist for the thirty seconds it takes to avoid conceding.

That isn't skepticism. It's [[Polylogism]]: one logic for the domains where he likes the results, another for the domain where he doesn't. He'd never let an engineer mix metric and imperial because consistency felt inconvenient that day. He'd never accept a physicist saying "cause and effect apply to falling rocks but not to falling rocks I'd rather were floating." Yet when the subject is [[Human action]], and specifically *his* preferred use of force against other people's property, consistency becomes optional. The contradiction was never between the libertarian and Hume. It's between the statist and himself.

## The Line in the Sand

Suppose you do meet the rare consistent one: the man who follows P8 all the way down and says, *fine, no oughts bind anyone, including the norms of reasoning. Contradictions don't bother me.*

You can't argue him out of that, and you shouldn't try. Every argument assumes the listener already cares about the [[Law of Non-Contradiction]], so it can't be what makes someone start caring. Aristotle made this point about the man who denies non-contradiction. If he says anything meaningful, he's using the law. If he says nothing, he is, in Aristotle's phrase, "no better than a plant." The consistent nihilist hasn't lost the argument. He's **left it**. What's left to him isn't discourse but the only other way humans settle conflicts: [[Jungle Law|force]]. That's the [[Primacy of Consciousness]] in its purest form: *reality, logic, and your property answer to what I want.*

It helps to be precise about who's actually on the other side of that line, because it isn't everyone who has ever contradicted themselves. A man who lied for his own benefit, noticed the contradiction, and changed his conduct is *inside* the line. That's what having the norm looks like. Correction is the norm working. The man outside the line isn't the one who commits contradictions. He's the one who adopts them as a standing policy and says "so what?" when they're pointed out. *Sure, that's true. I don't care.* Nothing in the universe physically stops a man from saying that. But notice it isn't a counter-argument. It's a refusal to make one, and the honest thing for someone who has made that refusal is to stop talking, because he has nothing left to say. One is a fallible reasoner. The other has resigned from reasoning and would like to keep the benefits of membership, starting with other people's stuff.

That's the fundamental divide, stated plainly. It isn't left versus right, or compassion versus greed. It's between people who accept that consistency binds them, even when it's inconvenient and even when the conclusion costs them something they want, and people who only accept it when it pays. With the first group, every disagreement is resolvable in principle, because both sides answer to the same standard. With the second, there's nothing to resolve. There's only a decision about how to live alongside them.

And libertarians already have the answer to that, without needing to convert anyone. You don't debate the man who has renounced reasons. You decline to share a [[Contracts|covenant]] with him, you defend your [[private property|property]] if he comes for it, and you let [[Law]] — the part of ethics that handles [[Conflicts]], nothing more — do the only job it was ever for. The line in the sand isn't a failure to persuade. It's the recognition that persuasion was only ever on offer to people who accept what persuasion requires. (See [[The Woke Precedent]] for why screening at the gate is ordinary, not exotic.)

## The Absurd Hill

Step back and notice how far down this went. It started with something as ordinary as *don't take other people's things*, a rule every child grasps and every thief expects to be applied when someone takes *his* things. To get around it, the statist had to go below economics, below ethics, below the norms of his own reasoning, all the way to the claim that nothing obliges anyone to anything. That's the hill. It's a very strange one to die on, because the only thing waiting at the top is someone else's property.

The libertarian doesn't need the whole philosophical stack to know stealing is wrong. He needs it only to show that the man denying it had to saw through the floor under his own feet to do so.
