The claim: a stateless society might work fine internally, but it can't field a standing army or centralized command the way a hostile neighboring state can — sooner or later an organized, collectivist state invades and simply conquers it, and the whole experiment ends the moment it meets real geopolitical pressure.

Conquering a state is efficient precisely because it's centralized: topple one government, seize its tax apparatus and command structure, and you inherit control over the entire population in one move — that's the actual military logic that makes invasion profitable. A stateless territory has no such capture point. There's no capital to take, no single leadership to decapitate, no tax base to seize and redirect — an invader has to subdue every armed, privately defended property individually, financed by an occupying force with no local tax revenue of its own to fund the occupation, against a population with every incentive (their own homes, funded by their own insurers and defense agencies) to resist rather than surrender on some distant government's behalf. This isn't hypothetical: guerrilla and militia-based resistance has historically been far costlier to occupy than centralized states with an army to defeat in one battle and a capital to march on. Removing the state doesn't remove the capacity to defend against invasion — it removes the single point of failure that makes conquest worth attempting in the first place. See [[Anarcho-Capitalism deters state formation]] for the same underlying logic applied to internal, rather than external, aggressors.
