The claim: without a regulator setting and enforcing limits, there's nothing stopping firms from polluting rivers, clear-cutting forests, and strip-mining resources into ruin.

Unowned and commonly-held resources are the ones that get trashed, not privately owned ones — that's the [[Tragedy of the commons]] in a sentence: when no individual can exclude others or capture the benefit of preserving a resource, nobody has an incentive to preserve it, and everybody has an incentive to extract from it before the next guy does. A river or airshed that's actually somebody's [[private property|property]] gets defended the same way any property gets defended — dumping waste into it is trespass and pollution against an owner, actionable the same as if you'd dumped it on their lawn, and courts have historically handled exactly this kind of tort before regulatory agencies displaced the common-law remedy with permits that let polluters buy a legal license to pollute up to a bureaucratically-set limit. The state's actual environmental record — state-owned commons, state-permitted dumping, state-run industries with zero profit-and-loss discipline — is worse than the property-rights alternative it claims to be protecting us from.
