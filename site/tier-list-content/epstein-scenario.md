The claim: without a regulator watching how the wealthy accumulate and use money, nothing stops someone with enough of it from building private infrastructure for trafficking and blackmail — "the market will punish it" isn't good enough when the harm is this severe and the victims have no power to punish anything.

Trafficking, exploitation of minors, and blackmail aren't things a market failure produces — they're [[Aggression|direct acts of aggression and fraud against specific, identifiable victims]], already crimes under the NAP with no additional "regulation of wealth" required to make them so; the objection is really asking what stops someone with money and connections from *evading enforcement* of laws that already exist, which is a different question with an inconvenient answer for the state model. The actual Epstein case is the strongest evidence against, not for, the "we need a regulator" premise: it happened under one of the most heavily regulated, well-funded law-enforcement and prosecutorial apparatuses in the world, and the failure wasn't a lack of rules on the books — it was a captured, monopoly enforcement and plea-deal system that a state's own prosecutors and political connections could quietly bend. A decentralized enforcement market, with no single agency to capture and reputations that competing firms have every incentive to expose rather than protect, removes exactly the chokepoint that let one well-connected operator buy his way past the "oversight" this objection is asking for more of.
