The claim: without a state to grant and enforce patents and copyright, nobody could stop competitors from immediately copying an invention or a work — innovation and creative output would be badly under-incentivized.

Homesteading answers this directly: [[Homesteading (First-comer Ethic)|the first person to think of something doesn't own the idea, because ideas aren't scarce]] — you copying a pattern I thought of doesn't remove my use of it the way you taking my apple removes mine. Property rights exist to resolve conflict over rivalrous, scarce resources; a pattern that can be copied infinitely without subtracting from the original creates no such conflict, which means "IP" isn't property, it's a state-granted monopoly on an *action* — the right to stop someone else from arranging their own ink, their own silicon, their own body, in a pattern you thought of first. Enforcing that requires aggressing against someone's actual, physical, scarce property to prevent them using it the way they choose. And empirically, innovation doesn't grind to a halt without it: first-mover advantage, brand reputation, trade secrets, and the sheer speed of continuing to out-innovate a copier all incentivize creation in industries — fashion, cuisine, open-source software — that get little or no IP protection today.
