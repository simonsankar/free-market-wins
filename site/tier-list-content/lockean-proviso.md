The claim: homesteading lets whoever gets there first claim unowned resources permanently, which means the first generation can homestead everything, leaving everyone born later with no unowned resources left to claim and no just claim to any of it — the "enough and as good left for others" condition on original acquisition never actually holds.

Someone has to own it, and every alternative to first-use is worse, not just less convenient — [[Homesteading (First-comer Ethic)|late-comer ethics, majority vote, and common ownership]] all either require force against whoever already improved the resource or collapse into paralysis, because you'd need every future person's consent before using anything at all. But the premise is also just false about how wealth actually works: almost nothing valuable in a modern economy is "unclaimed land waiting to be found" — it's produced, and production isn't zero-sum the way the "left for others" framing assumes. A person born today doesn't inherit a smaller slice of a fixed pie; they inherit access to capital, technology, and a division of labor built by generations of prior homesteaders and traders that makes them vastly wealthier than any "fair share" of unowned dirt ever could. The proviso treats property as a grabbed inheritance instead of what it actually is — the result of someone doing something with a thing nobody else was doing anything with.
