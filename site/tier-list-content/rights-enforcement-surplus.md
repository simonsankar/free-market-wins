The claim: switching your protection agency isn't like switching your grocery store — once one firm has boots on the ground in your neighborhood and the relationships with local courts and insurers, it becomes a captive local monopoly free to raise prices, since leaving means starting over from zero.

The switching cost is real but smaller than the one it's being compared against: leaving your current, single, non-optional protection agency — the state — means leaving the country, forfeiting your property, and finding somewhere else on Earth that will let you in. A competitive protection market is a strict improvement on that baseline even with imperfect exit, because "imperfect exit" beats "no exit" every time, and because the agency itself has a captive stake in *not* becoming visibly extortionate — reputation and insurer/arbitrator relationships are the product, and a firm known for gouging customers loses the cross-agency trust that makes its services worth anything, the same discipline that already keeps [[Contracts|reputation-based enforcement]] like historical merchant law honest without a state. Compare that to the actual status quo: a police and court monopoly that already faces zero competitive pressure, can't be fired, and taxes you whether you use it or not — the "captive monopoly" objection describes the system it's defending far more precisely than the one it's attacking.
